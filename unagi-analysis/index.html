Team Unagi at ICFPC 2026: A Detailed Analysis

An engineering study of icfpc-unagi/icfpc2026, compared throughout with team wheezards’ own contest · prepared by the wheezards tooling agent, 2026-08-05

Abstract

Team Unagi — six-time-class ICFPC winners — published their complete ICFP Programming Contest 2026 repository. We cloned it (779 commits, 384 MB, 6 contributors) and read it end to end: every per-problem postmortem trail (~230 documents), the toolchain crates (~45k lines of Rust), the autonomous-agent systems, and the operational documentation. This report reconstructs, in detail: (1) their approach to every one of the sixteen graded problems, (2) their approach to the contest as an operation, and (3) the machinery behind each stage of their pipeline — simulation, generation, composition, optimization, verification, submission, and knowledge management.

Every comparison with our own team (wheezards) is anchored to one moment: our archived standings snapshot of 2026-07-27 08:38 UTC (~3.5 hours before the end, pre-freeze), the only timestamp at which both teams’ numbers are directly comparable. At that moment Unagi was rank 11 of 268 with 29.31 points; we were rank 39 with 24.68.

The one-sentence conclusion: Unagi ran a three-layer factory — algorithms chosen and re-chosen by strong humans, machines emitted by per-problem generators over a verified gadget discipline, and a generic annealing optimizer polishing valid artifacts — on cloud infrastructure that existed before the contest began, with LLM agents embedded as scheduled, leaderboard-driven workers inside that factory. The 4.6-point gap between the teams is concentrated almost entirely in six problems where their algorithm was a different complexity class from ours; where the fight was layout and operations, we were within small factors and once ahead.

The contest in one page

ICFPC 2026 (“the basics of real-world systems programming”) ran 2026-07-24 12:00 UTC → 2026-07-27 12:00 UTC. Teams programmed the littleman machine: a 2-D ASCII grid of rooms connected by pipes, in which little men walk over instruction cells. Key mechanics that shape everything below:

  • A man walks in a heading; blank cells preserve it; arrows change it; instruction cells execute (M/W register moves, arithmetic, s/r pipe I/O, branches X/d/a/x, literals in backticks, H halt).
  • r/s bind to the nearest pipe endpoint (Manhattan, tie by coordinates) — where an instruction stands determines which pipe it talks to. Geometry is an operand.
  • Pipes are FIFOs; pipe length = latency AND capacity. Backtick literals pair per row and per column — vertical alignment of unrelated literals can create illegal pairs.
  • Displays (LM-75) take address/data/swap streams; some problems are scored on emitted frames.
  • Score (most problems) = max(width, height)² × average ticks — lower is better. Footprint is squared, so the long side dominates; ticks stop counting at the last correct output.
  • Points per problem = cases-passed fraction + rank fraction (2.0 max); only a team’s best submission counts, so submitting is free and floors are valuable.

That is the central lesson of this study, and it is also, almost verbatim, the doctrine written at the top of Unagi’s own technique catalog: “most problems are footprint-tick scored; the two axes often move in opposite directions; always judge by the product” — their catalog’s preamble, our claude_44, independently converged.

Part I — Their Approach to the Contest

1. The team and the division of labor

Six members, all veterans: Takuya Akiba (iwiwi), Kentaro Imajo (imos), Hiroaki Iwami (sulume), Yoichi Iwata (wata), Toshiki Kataoka, Naohiro Takahashi (tos). The commit record (779 commits) shows a clean split:

member commits primary territory inferred role
Akiba 323 iwiwi/ (8,613 files) autonomous agent systems v1→v4; post-contest editorial
sulume 202 solutions/ (659 files touched) solution iteration and postmortems
Imajo 132 crates/ (692), docs/ (634) infrastructure, optimizer, documentation system
Kataoka 62 crates/, tos/, problems/ tooling
Iwata 49 solutions/ (453 files) hand-crafted flagship solvers (wata-orz artifacts)
Takahashi 7 tos/ diaries artisan solving with plain-text diaries

Daily commit cadence: 88 (day 1) → 269 → 280 → 135 (final half-day) — sustained parallel work, not a final-day crunch.

Three personal workflows coexist under shared conventions: Akiba’s agent loops, wata’s prompt-driven sessions with a human handling git, tos’s plain-text diaries (tos/gradebook-diary.txt, memory-diary.txt, sort-numbers-diary.txt) plus snapshot scripts. The shared convention that binds them is the score-named artifact: solutions/<slug>/<score>.man + <score>.md + optionally <score>.py (generator). “The current best is the numerically smallest score” is literally how wata’s agent prompt defines the interface.

2. Preparation: the contest before the contest

docs/preparation.md records the doctrine, distilled from their 2023–2025 repos (kept as reference clones): “make every task-independent thing work in advance, including one full rehearsal on a past problem.” Ported and verified green before the start:

  • A Rust workspace split by dependency into unagi-util / gcp / sql / lock / executor / www plus contest-specific crates to be added later.
  • GCP: buckets (gs://icfpc2026, gs://icfpc2026-data), service account, Cloud SQL + phpMyAdmin, Artifact Registry, Cloud Run deploy targets via make docker/<service>@deploy, billing alerts — and a GCE quota request filed early (they used 224-vCPU machines in 2023; quota raises have lead time).
  • Secrets bootstrapped from a single UNAGI_PASSWORD: AES-256-CBC encrypted configs committed in-repo, webhook URLs and API keys fetched at runtime from GCS, never committed and never logged. Repo-local gcloud/gsutil wrappers install a project-scoped Cloud SDK under .cache/ on first use — no system-wide installs.
  • SAT solvers (cadical, rustsat-*) pre-declared as opt-in workspace dependencies in case the task wanted them.
  • AGENTS.md — standing rules for LLM development agents: minimal diffs, tests + make lint after Rust changes, never print secrets, GCP mutations require explicit human approval, destructive operations forbidden, update docs when behavior changes.

3. Running services during the contest

docs/services.md and docs/api/submissions.md describe a small production system:

  • server (Cloud Run, custom domain icfpc.sx9.jp, Basic auth): the human dashboard — tasks, locks, communications.
  • api (Cloud Run): the submission relay. ./bin/submit authenticates with UNAGI_PASSWORD; the service stores each program as versioned JSON in GCS, keeps a persistent queue + state + event history in Cloud SQL, and is the only place that reads the official contest API key (from GCS, cached 5 minutes in-process). No member laptop ever holds the key. A Cloud Scheduler cron drives process-one queue steps.
  • system: periodic jobs; Discord webhooks for notifications (URL fetched at runtime, never embedded).
  • unagi-executor: the DB-backed task executor that fans work out across a fleet of GCE machines — carried over from previous years.
  • A scoreboard binary reads their own submission history and the official standings through the www API and re-runs public cases through the local simulator — continuous like-for-like scoring.

Compare us: one coordinator agent (Claude) held the API key locally and was the single submitter; judging was a local script (subdb.py, preflight.py); coordination was git-mediated messages. Functionally similar shape — gatekeeper + queue — but theirs is a deployed service with history, versioning, and no key exposure, built years earlier.

4. The strategy layer

Four patterns recur everywhere in their repo and deserve names:

  1. Expected-gain-proportional attention. The v2 scheduler samples the next problem to work on with weight 2 − Unagi's current points on that problem (2.0 being the per-problem maximum). Modes improve and rewrite are chosen 50/50; the target is a randomly chosen team above them (or their own best when ranked 1). The explore half — rewrite — is what produced the ≥100× jumps.
  2. Floors first, ceilings second. For llm they shipped a public-case memorizer (14/28 cases, honest filename 14-of-28) as a floor while the real universal interpreter was being built. Cases-passed is half the points; a floor is nearly free.
  3. Leaderboard-coupled everything. Postmortems quote the leader’s score and the gap (“gap to leader 6.8%”); one artifact records the policy “commit/push only after reaching rank 1”; the v3 agent loop polls standings before every run and sleeps while rank 1.
  4. Everything regenerable, everything scored. Artifacts are named by official score; generators + seeds are committed next to outputs and byte-reproduce them; placements ship PNG renders and JSON routing reports; experiments that FAILED are written down with numbers (memory’s 2-worker rotation: “no average gain, heavy consecutive-access cases regress” — recorded, rejected).

5. The agent systems: v1 → v2 → v3

Three generations inside one 72-hour contest:

  • v1 (from 17:32 UTC day 1): flat timestamped work dirs, one-shot Codex runs per problem.
  • v2: three roles. Solvers (agent sessions humans launch in tmux) ask a Scheduler for work — an SQLite service holding free-form suggestions (FIFO, from humans or the Curator) and falling back to the expected-gain sampling above; a Curator — deliberately a single writer — wakes every 300 s, reads new solver memos, integrates them into per-problem memory and a cross-problem Cookbook, and posts cross-pollination suggestions back to the scheduler.
  • v3: the mature loop (loop.sh <slug>, loop_v2.py). Each run launches a fresh-context agent that inherits the durable tree: problems/<slug>/ (current position + solution history), knowledge/ (cross-problem techniques), work/ (scratch). The solver prompt is a six-step pipeline with concrete shell per step — assess (auth, ff-only pull, resolve the problem via the public API) → solve → submit → record → notify → save. loop_v2.py fetches standings before each run and sets the success criterion dynamically: catch rank 1; if more than 10× behind, achieve ~10×; if rank 1, sleep 10 minutes and re-check. A semantic-regressions.json records meaning-level mistakes past agents made, as a regression suite for prompts.
  • v4-hand: the human pair — wata + iwiwi trading artifacts on the hardest interpreter problems (LLLM’s descent 3.8T → 68.97B is a filename trail alternating _iwiwi / _wata-orz), with detailed architecture notes (LLLM_BITPLANE_PIPELINE.md etc.).

Contrast with ours: we ran 4–8 heterogeneous agents (claude, codex, gpt, chatgpt_1/2/4, codex_3, alexey) coordinated by a human + one coordinator agent over git-messages, with manual task (re)assignment. Our topology excelled at handoffs — ten of our fourteen final-day improvements were other agents’ finished work that only needed judging and pushing. Theirs excelled at unattended grinding against the leaderboard — fresh contexts, durable knowledge, machine-checkable goals. The two models are complementary, and neither team ran the other’s.

Part II — The Machinery Behind Each Stage

Their pipeline, stage by stage, with the exact artifact that implements each. The through-line: at every stage, the unit of work is a regenerable, testable, scored artifact — never a hand-edited grid.

Stage 0: Ground truth — unagi-simulator + conformance

~10k lines of Rust. Values are i64 with the official wrapping semantics; the mid-contest Y (split) instruction is implemented; program boards are flat row-major byte arrays.

The decisive design choice is dual execution: ./bin/simulate runs the Rust engine, ./bin/simulate-official runs the organizers’ own Go/WASM build behind the same CLI, and both consume a shared conformance file (crates/simulator/testdata/cases.json). Divergences between spec and official behavior are cataloged in docs/simulator/language-conformance.md, and recorded probes of the live editor sit in docs/official/editor-observations-2026-07-26.json. Solution harnesses routinely ship both verifiers (snake: verify.rs and verify-official.mjs; llm cross-checks its pipe shifter and guest ALU against the WASM).

Ours, for contrast: sim.py + C extension + Rust executor, with the official WASM added as a judge only on day 3 — after an unimplemented Y and a wrong wall rule had each cost us a submission. Same final architecture; theirs was doctrine from hour one, ours a repair.

Stage 1: Algorithm selection — humans, with a rewrite budget

No tool chooses algorithms; people do, and the operation forces the question twice: the scheduler’s 50/50 improve/rewrite modes, and postmortem templates that open with “computation graph / pseudocode” before any layout detail. Algorithm changes are tracked as first-class events: pathfinder has three distinct generations in its artifact trail, matmul two, subset-sum two.

Stage 2: Composition — gadgets with contracts

Two implementations of the same discipline:

  • crates/hand (~9.9k lines): gadgets as Rust async actors — typed ports over channels (OutPort/InPort), a room’s behavior unit-tested against its boundary trace via assert_room (with tick limits), auto_layout for placement, a display model. Entire solutions are emitted by binaries (cargo run -p hand --bin subsetsum2 -- --layout out.man) and byte-compared against the committed artifact. The regression tests encode their scars: false pipe connections at wall-adjacent bends; collisions after the final output.
  • chokudai/compiler v0: a hardware-CAD flow. Input: a flat physical netlist of gadget instances + port connections. Passes: port/protocol validation → dependency-graph layer placement → A pipe routing with fixed terminal flow → nearest-port contract check* (does every s/r/q still bind to the pipe the netlist meant?) → emit .man. Implicit fanout is rejected. The gadget catalog (chokudai/gadgets/) holds verified parts — crossovers, persistent counted adders/mergers, phase gates, relays, a matmul PE — each with port order, backpressure and tick behavior recorded.

chokudai/README.md states the doctrine that connects them: artisan small problems → harvest verified gadgets → compose large problems from gadgets via generators → fuse hot combinations back into larger artisan macros → reuse. Decide per problem: one dominant computation → artisan; several independently verifiable subsystems → compose.

Stage 3: Generation — the per-solution compilers

Every serious artifact ships its generator: <score>.py (or .rs) plus, on the biggest machines, _router.py, _compact.py, _finalize.py, a placement PNG, and a JSON routing report. Generators own the problems we fought by hand: literal packing (history-lesson’s generator verifies every 64-bit literal and aligns widths so no accidental vertical backtick pair can form), nearest-pipe preservation (matmul’s router validates 40 nearest-pipe sites against the donor: “the nearest-pipe identity as seen from each room is part of the ABI”), routing search (snake’s router records seed 364 — routing is a searched, reproducible artifact).

The postmortems repeatedly separate “computation graph identical; layout only” steps from algorithm steps — the two are different artifacts with different verification obligations.

Stage 4: Optimization — unagi-optimizer (+optimizer_cli, ~21.8k lines)

optimize <slug> <valid.man> --time-limit --seed [--submit] — local search over an already-valid program:

Solution (.man) → Program → Layout (rooms/pipes, read-only view)
      ↑                                      ↓
 accept/reject ← EvaluationBackend ← Modifier::propose
      ↖—————————— Search (annealing/greedy) ——————↙
  • Modifiers (plugin trait, stable IDs): trim-empty, room-shrink, room-move, pipe-reroute, relocate, room-deadcell, room-op-slide, room-commute-swap, room-route-nudge, room-corridor-reroute, room-op-corridor-reroute (carries pure register ops along a rerouted corridor while preserving heading sequence and visited-cell count; s/r/q deferred until nearest-pipe-invariance checks land).
  • Safety model: every modifier is tagged changes_timing(); a differential equivalence gate (--equiv-gate) is enforced for timing-changing modifiers only — their systematization of the “pipe length is latency AND capacity; never shorten a storage pipe” trap that we learned by deadlock.
  • Evaluation: public cases in parallel (Rayon), result order stable; the search core is pure (injected clock, event stream) so it tests without files or wall time.
  • Runner: progress events, best-artifact saving, submission with backoff — it can run unattended and submit its own improvements.
  • The roadmap document ranks candidate modifiers by measured criteria: leverage (footprint = max(w,h)² ⇒ only a fully cleared perimeter band pays; neutral moves are still valuable as enablers), valid-yield, firing frequency (measured per problem: one modifier fired zero times on snake’s best — deprioritized), timing class. An interior survey quantifies the opportunity: best answers are “extremely roomy” — snake ~11.4% dense, gradebook ~6.8%, with the blank space inside one giant room.

Measured contribution on visible artifacts: pathfinder 81.7B → 80.9B, matmul 500M → 495.8M (~1% each), with better-scored optimizer outputs kept as candidates for gradebook. The pattern: generators and algorithm changes earn the orders of magnitude; the optimizer converts idle CPU into steady percent-scale gains.

Ours, for contrast: this tool is nearly clause-for-clause the “manifest” our team dictated mid-contest (a randomized, high-performance Rust room optimizer applying semantics-preserving transformations). They had it running by 07:43 UTC on day 3; we wrote the idea down at roughly that hour and built the analogous machinery (room contracts, then post-contest room_ir/room_place/ room_compile) after the end.

Stage 5: Reading machines — crates/mandis

A disassembler/decompiler: validated .man → Rust-like pseudocode with goto labels, room/pipe numbering, source coordinates, and static per-block tick counts. MVP by their own description, but it closes the loop: machines are readable, so foreign or old artifacts can be understood, priced, and re-entered into the generator flow. (Our room_ir is the same idea pointed at re-emission.)

Stage 6: Scoring, submission, records

  • crates/scorer — the official formula over public tests (./bin/score --offline --compact), used as the acceptance gate in every postmortem.
  • The submission relay (Part I §3): queue, versioned storage, event history, single holder of the API key; plus crates/submit’s scoreboard binary re-scoring everything like-for-like.
  • Every postmortem records: result, local + official submission IDs, SHA-256 of the artifact, geometry, tick statistics, what changed versus the donor, and a verification checklist.

Stage 7: Knowledge — catalog, textbook, editorial

  • docs/techniques/README.md — a 56-entry reverse-lookup catalog (“what do I reach for?”), each entry: one-line summary, mechanism, cost/benefit, named example solutions. Its preamble is the scoring physics (judge by footprint² × ticks product; footprint-only problems excepted).
  • docs/textbook/ — the same knowledge re-indexed by goal (part 3 techniques, part 4 construction patterns), 1:1 links to the catalog.
  • docs/official/ — bilingual mirrors of every official document, plus recorded editor-behavior probes.
  • iwiwi/editorial/ — a post-contest interactive site (Next.js): each explanation descends algorithm → computation graph → pseudocode → the real manifest, with hover-highlighted regions and “guided gadget execution” that steps a chosen gadget with live room state. Explanation is treated as a first-class engineering artifact with its own authoring guide.

A selection of catalog entries that name things we met the hard way: #17 Geometry Is the Operand (nearest-pipe binding), #30 Fixed-Width Aligned Literals (vertical backtick control), #34 Keep Scored Snapshots, #38 Phase Capacity of a Shared Loop (lap/gcd(lap,T) ≥ N), #42 Latency-Preserving Fold, #44 Cursor-Relative Ring, #47 Epoch Tag Instead of Clearing, #56 Differential Dynamic Profiling. And entries we never had: #36 Y Fan-Out, #37 Y Delay-Line Sort, #48 Temporal Register Aliasing, #52 Multiply-Then-Shift Classifier.

Part III — Problem by Problem

Sixteen graded problems. For each: what the problem asks, Unagi’s algorithm and machine, how the artifact was produced and verified, the score trail, and the comparison with our solution. Scores are official unless marked public. “Snapshot” = 2026-07-27 08:38 UTC standings.

pathfinder — 141× (their 114B vs our 16.1T at snapshot)

Problem. A 16×16 maze arrives as 256 wall bits plus a robot start; flags appear one per round; guide the robot to each flag and draw its path on a 16×16 display.

Their trail — three architectures in 48 hours:

Generation 1 — “four-lane bit-wave BFS” (80.9T, 18/18, 3783×3812, 9.0 MB of text). Algorithmically already advanced — walls packed into sixteen 16-bit row masks, reverse BFS from each goal by bitwise wave propagation in four parallel row lanes, parent directions recorded, a streaming walker replays the path to the display. But the machine was enormous; at box 3812 the footprint term buried it.

Generation 2 — “16-row parallel BFS” (62.2T, 3456×1077), generated by a Rust program (62166740306242.67.rs) that emits basic blocks, branches, a local router — and a bus protocol: the CPU sends target, action, payload triples over shared pipes; every room self-selects by target ID (entry sequence rM8W- tests “is this for target 8?” in five cells); row actors answer a probe with the single value 2*(16*y+x) + is_open, folding the branch result and coordinates into one integer so the CPU never re-reads metadata per direction. Board rows are 64-bit words at 4 bits/cell (wall, visited, 2-bit predecessor); probes go in fixed U→R→D→L priority.

Generation 3 — “packed row-bitset reverse BFS” (681B → 211B → 141.7B → 133.7B → 132.7B → 121.7B; sides 238 → 231 → 222). The redesign that mattered. One 64-bit word per maze row:

state[row] = available | parent_low<<16 | parent_high<<32 | frontier<<48

A 16-word ring holds immutable free-cell masks and resets the dynamic words each round (epoch-style, no clearing pass). The BFS expands the frontier by whole-row bit operations and scans only the row span the frontier occupies, ±1 — U/R/D/L candidate masks consume available in priority order so equal-distance ties resolve deterministically. During reconstruction, robot and flag positions are packed into one integer and arrival is tested modulo 65537. Machine ~222×222, official average ~1.78M ticks. Each step ships generator + finalizer + router scripts and a placement PNG; the final 5.65% came from a layout-only change (a 9-register bank moved 2 cells left, “instruction streams byte-identical, all 38 pipe endpoints preserved”). Their optimizer adds a final ~1% (81.7B → 80.9B artifacts).

Ours. A per-cell walking BFS in a 181×936 room (the machine’s whole height); contest improvements were geometric (3.0× then 1.227× from snake line-merges) landing at 4.37T. Our post-contest measurement explains the residual: 90.8% of our room-0 walk was inter-operation transport under nearest-pipe lane structure — but even a perfect re-layout of our machine cannot reach their numbers, because their frontier step is O(rows), ours O(cells). The gap decomposes roughly as: algorithm ~40×, machine size ~4×.

matmul — 356× (16.7M vs 5.93B)

Problem. Multiply an N×M by an M×K matrix (dimensions bounded by 16), all values streamed.

Their trail. Early: a generated 16×16 processing-element grid (kept as answer.man + generator, marked legacy) — scores descending 685M → 446M. Then the REDESIGN: a scalar ring-stream —

for i in 0..N:  sums[0..K] = 0
  for t in 0..M: for j in 0..K: sums[j] += A[i,t] * B[t,j]
  emit sums[0..K]

realized as: A expanded elementwise ×K in (i,t,j) order; B circulating row-major in a pipe ring; the K partial sums circulating in a FIFO whose storage is an exact 261-cell pipe — the pipe’s length is simultaneously its capacity and the loop’s phase; a row-marker stream (M−1 zeros then 1) delimits rows. Ten rooms, fifteen pipes. This dropped them 446M → 96M → 76M → 36.8M (50×47).

The final 15% (36.8M → 31.1M, 46×46) is a pure placement compaction with the topology frozen: a Rust placement generator, four left-boundary crop passes, one local room move, and a router that re-verifies 40 nearest-pipe sites against the donor — their postmortem’s phrase: “the nearest-pipe identity as seen from each room is part of the ABI.” The input pipe alone was shortened 10 → 2 cells (−8 ticks average); every other exact pipe length preserved.

Verification. Exhaustive: all 3,375 dimension triples, 20,250 boundary-value cases, 4,096 random, 23 targeted; byte-reproduction of the artifact; the harness committed.

Ours. 5.93B from a hand-architecture with a 1.42× squeeze — we never revisited the algorithm. Both of their jumps were closed to us: the ring-stream (algorithm) and the frozen-topology compaction (we had no re-placer; our post-contest compiler is exactly that tool).

subset-sum — 37,601× (995M vs 37.4T)

Problem. Choose a subset of ≤20 integers summing to a target; output the count and the chosen values in original order; lexicographically smallest index set among solutions.

Their algorithm — meet-in-the-middle, in pipes. Split indices into even/odd halves (≤10 each ⇒ ≤1024 subsets per half). Each subset is ONE 64-bit record: subset sum in the high bits; the low 40 bits interleave a lexicographic priority (index i at bit 39−i) and the selection mask (index i at bit i) — constructed so that among equal sums, the numerically largest record IS the lexicographically smallest index set. Ordering logic becomes plain integer comparison.

The two halves’ subset lists are generated as descending sequences merged in pipe loops: duplicate and merge rooms merge “current sequence” with “sequence + next contribution” on the fly (left branch adds positive contributions; right branch subtracts from the target). decode unpacks; match two-pointer-walks the two descending streams; max keeps the best matched record; select replays the archived input values against the selection bits, emitting count + values in original order.

The machine. 106×106, 17 rooms, 25 pipes; the MITM lists live in snake pipes with exact capacities — left-a0 529 cells, left-a1 517, right-a1 540, right-a0 512, all ≥ the required 512 records, one snake detouring around a room “to make up missing capacity”. Central pipe bands are documented column by column. Generated by crates/hand (--bin subsetsum2), byte-compared; 13 unit tests + 25 hand library tests; each public case run 118 ticks past the final output to prove no post-answer wall collision. They also submitted a trimmed and an untrimmed (blank-margined) version and observed the SAME official score — establishing that the scorer normalizes surrounding whitespace (an experiment, recorded).

Trail. 10.4B → 9.2B → 9.0B → 690M → 669M → 531M → 443M (their repo best; snapshot 995M was mid-descent). 31 postmortems — their most-iterated problem.

Ours. Direct enumeration; 37.4T. No layout work recovers a complexity-class difference; this single problem is ~1 point of standing.

sudoku-validity — 3,709× (2.50M vs 9.29B)

Problem. Validate 9×9 Sudoku boards streamed cell by cell; emit 1/0 per board.

Their machine — 28×28, branchless. All 27 constraint groups (9 rows, 9 cols, 9 boxes) are 9-bit used-masks packed into FOUR signed 64-bit words, with each group’s field placed so the word’s sign bit stays free (word 0: rows 0–6; word 1: cols 0–4 + rows 7–8; word 2: cols 5–8 + top boxes; word 3: middle + bottom boxes). An input digit v becomes 1 << (v−1) shifted into its group’s field.

Four holder rooms sit in the corners, each looping R ~ W - s: receive candidate, new = old XOR candidate, store, reply old − new — which is negative iff the bit was previously unused. The central aggregator ANDs the row/col/box replies: all-negative ⇒ still valid. Aggregation gates the next candidate broadcast, so state updates can never be overtaken — lockstep by protocol, not by timing luck.

Layout notes show nearest-pipe-aware micro-routing: a reply path was shortened 17 → 15 cells, and the postmortem explains why it could not be shortened further — the next bend would put a generator’s s closer to the wrong pipe. Verified with 527 differential tests (27 single-duplicate archetypes + 500 deterministic-random boards) against a reference implementation.

Trail. 2.88M → 2.86M → 2.73M → 2.63M → 2.50M.

Ours. 9.29B (later 6.74B): a sequential checker walking per-cell logic. Their entire validation runs in ~3,153 average ticks on a 28×28 footprint; ours is both larger and thousands of times slower — pure algorithm (bit-packing + sign-classification).

plotter — 271× (3.72M vs 1.01B)

Problem. Execute drawing commands (pen moves/lines) on the LM-75 display; scored per round on emitted frames.

Their machine — 50×50, 13 rooms. Architecture held fixed from the F=58 generation; two engineering moves did the rest:

  1. Literal→arithmetic so code can bend. A backtick literal is straight-line by definition — it cannot turn a corner. Replacing `32` with 5M1{ (set 5, shift 1 by it) and `4096` with 3M4*M makes the instruction stream foldable, at which point every room was comb-folded (“fold4”: a w×4 box yields 4w−8 op slots vs a ring’s 2W+2H−8). Thirteen rooms re-folded with before/after tables (Iterator 19×7→9×7 with the 8/10-cell hot loop untouched — only cold init/teardown paths were folded; Writer 15×6→10×6; etc.).
  2. “Plan R”: the floor as a planar graph. They stated the invariant first — a horizontal pipe must cut vertical channels — then chose the ROOM ORDER so that exactly one net (pYM) crosses the north-south divide, and 19 pipes routed on the first attempt. The previous generation’s blocking crossing (D→Y over K→F) was removed by reordering rooms, not by cleverer routing. All pipe lengths are listed in the postmortem; six shrank dramatically (pDV 69→3).

Underneath sits a measured cost model: round ticks = 142 + 8.4 × pixels (previous generation: 156 + 8.5p), so footprint 58→50 (58²/50² = 1.35×) was correctly identified as the dominant term. Then they measured per-pipe criticality empirically — lengthen each pipe by +8 cells, observe the public-average delta — producing a tick/cell price list (pDX 3.5/cell … pVF 0, non-critical), and noted the two initialization critical paths are balanced (X branch 40 vs Y branch 42): “max applies — shortening only one is wasted.” A redesign/DESIGN.md carries the timing constraints checked at generation time.

Trail. 23.96M → 23.92M → 5.46M (F=58) → 5.05M → 3.87M (F=50); they logged the then-leader (3.627M) and their 6.8% gap.

Ours. 1.01B (958M final) from a hand machine + moves; our plotter notes measured the same phenomenon (1,642 blank runs; delay realized as distance) but the counted-loop fix stayed unbuilt.

gradebook — 116× at snapshot (405M vs 47.1B; both improved later)

Problem. Maintain a roster of N students × K grades under batches of GET/SET/AVG/TOP operations.

Their machine — 83×89, a circulating roster. The roster is a fixed-width FIFO ring [−N, id, g1..g4] × N (grades zero-padded to width 4); the negative record −N is both the count and the rotation marker, and every operation rotates the ring back to the marker, so each op starts aligned at a record boundary. GET/SET rotate and read/replace; AVG streams the selected grade column into an accumulator and emits floor(sum/N); TOP streams (grade, id) pairs through a GradeComparator → MinimumIdKeeper (a persistent compare-and-keep actor — catalog #26).

The committed improvement is pure packaging: TOP/AVG support actors replaced by smaller tiles, then coordinate compression — empty columns inside the main room and empty rows/columns globally deleted one at a time, “re-running the real simulator to 7/7 after every deletion” — 92×94 → 83×89. The artifact ships a regeneration script plus donor manifest. Optimizer artifacts dated 07-26 07:43/07:55 UTC carry 167.9M — the unattended polisher finding further compression on the same architecture.

Ours. Our gradebook win was the same family of insight — a result-collector confirmation pipe cutting ticks 95,493 → 73,931 (1.355×) — but executed on a 379-wide machine; theirs is ~4× smaller in footprint with ~5× fewer ticks. Catalog cross-links: #42 latency-preserving fold, #43 semantic NOP blanking, #56 differential dynamic profiling (find hot instructions by diffing two runs with different op counts).

Part III (continued) — The Interpreter Family and Snake

The three “little man” self-hosting problems asked teams to build littleman machines that execute littleman programs. These reward interpreter architecture above all — dispatch cost, memory representation, display protocol — and they are where Unagi’s human-pair (wata + iwiwi) worked deepest.

little-little-man (llm) — 545× (16.1T vs 8.78Q)

Problem. Execute an arbitrary guest littleman program (16×16 room, up to 3 men, pipes, full instruction set) and render each tick to the LM-75 display. 28 cases: 14 public, 14 private.

Solution A — the strategic floor: a public-case memorizer. A Rust generator (14-of-28.rs) emits a 282×274 machine that recognizes which public case it is running (broadcaster → 14 case-source rooms matching W, H and one identifying cell) and replays embedded frame diffs through a rasterizer + display adapter. Result: public 14/14, private 0/14 → 14/28 cases, score null (no footprint-tick score without a full pass), filed under the honest name 14-of-28. Under points = cases-fraction + rank-fraction, half the cases is a real floor, shipped while the real interpreter was built. They verified all 135 embedded frames against official data.

Solution B — the universal banked interpreter (958×713, 28/28, 16.09T). A littleman machine emulating a littleman machine:

  • the guest’s 16×16 grid lives in a 256-cell circulating memory;
  • guest room/man/pipe metadata occupy three 64-cell circulating memories plus 39 small scalar holder rooms;
  • a central controller executes up to 3 guest men in lockstep, implementing ^ > v <, digits, M + - X s r H with signed-64 semantics;
  • guest pipes update tail-first, reproducing the official same-tick cascade; send/receive blocking, nearest-endpoint selection and reading-order tie-breaks are computed at runtime from coordinates — the interpreter re-derives the binding rules we all fought, per guest step;
  • initialization paints the full frame; each round commits exactly one frame of diffs.

The geometry work is its own story: the controller’s command fan to ~50 service rooms was compressed from 180 columns to 152 by shifting the service column 15 rows and re-grading every command pipe’s slope so none cross; reply pipes drop into 2-row gaps under each service and merge into a 7-column vertical collector (replacing 50 independent return tracks); the 256-cell ring folds to 29×16. Verified by unit gadget tests plus pipe-shifter and guest-ALU cross-checks against the official WASM; official average ~15.9M ticks.

Ours. Also an interpreter (28/28) — at 8.78 QUADRILLION. The 545× is pure constant factor: our guest-step dispatch and memory walks cost orders of magnitude more ticks, and our machine was larger. Their 39 scalar holders + banked rings + lockstep controller is a articulated answer to exactly the constant-factor question.

little-little-little-man (lllm) — 4.4× (4.81B vs 21.2B)

Problem. A reduced interpreter-of-interpreter task (guest programs from a constrained subset; 21 cases).

Their trail. The LLLM/ directory preserves a hill-climb as <uuid>_<score>_<author>.man artifacts alternating between iwiwi and wata: 3.822T → 423B → 187B → 103.7B → 75.98B → 69.1B → 68.97B; the final redesign lands in little-little-little-man/ at 11.35B (21/21, 130×172, official avg 383,637 ticks) — and the snapshot shows 4.81B, i.e. they kept descending after the last committed postmortem.

Final architecture — “direct scalar service + pipelined controller”. The guest program is stored as 5-bit opcodes in a 260-element cursor-relative FIFO (catalog #44: rotate a logical head pointer, never move the data); execution state sits in an 8-element direct scalar service; the controller talks to exactly four subsystems: program memory, scalars, input, display writer. The final committed 8.77% came from packaging: the init loop’s 15 recurring “program request → display DATA” pairs laid out on ONE row, the remaining control flow shelf-packed beneath, then 49 empty controller rows deleted.

Ours. 21.2B at snapshot, 17.6B final — our strongest showing among the interpreter problems (a subagent’s eight-row shrink 303×311 → 303×303 with the merge+rail technique). Their advantage here was sustained human-pair iteration; the architectures rhyme (both ring memories with cursor state), and this is the problem family where our approach came closest on merit.

snake — 7.6× (107M vs 809M)

Problem. Play snake on a 16×16 board: fruit spawns, direction changes arrive, body grows; draw every state; detect self-collision and wall exit.

Their machine — 85×85. The snake body IS a physical FIFO of cell addresses, tail→head. Five scalar “echo holder” rooms keep dir / fruit / head / len / c16; the controller keeps the candidate head in register B and the remaining scan count in the backpack; self-collision = one full lap of the body FIFO comparing addresses. Drawing is incremental (catalog #54): head pixel + tail pixel per move with SWAP 1 preserve, plus fruit on spawn; round init draws once.

Toolchain per artifact (the fullest example in the repo): generate-up.py (generator) + router_hotloop.py with recorded seed 364 (routing is a searched, reproducible artifact) + verify.rs AND verify-official.mjs (both engines) + generate-random.py / generate-stress.py (fuzzers) + final.png (placement render). The committed best (234M) documents shrinking the Incremental Writer 13 → 10 columns and the controller’s bottom margin 6 → 2 rows, re-routing in the narrower room with two manual control-edge reroutes; optimizer artifacts carry 157M; snapshot 107M.

Ours. 809M — a 1.05× line-merge on an inherited machine. Theirs is what the same problem looks like when the machine is generated: every shrink is a re-run, not a surgery.

Part III (continued) — The Small-Machine Problems

Where machines fit in tens of cells per side, the competition is protocol design and single-cell placement. These are the problems where our numbers were closest to theirs — and once, better.

brackets — 4.5× (83.8K vs 377K)

Problem. Stream a bracket string; output 0 if balanced, else the 1-based position of the first error.

Their machine — 16×16, four stages.

  1. Feeder (lower-left): drops the length header, forwards each byte, appends a 0 end-marker.
  2. Classifier (upper-left): computes (5 × ASCII) >> 5 — one multiply and one out-of-range-safe shift map all six bracket characters onto three type codes {6, 14, 19} (catalog #52), with closers negated.
  3. Worker (lower-right): the WHOLE stack is one base-3 number in the off hand (catalog #7): push = stack*3 + type; pop = subtract the type and divide by 3 — a nonzero remainder is a type mismatch, a negative quotient is a pop-on-empty (catalog #8: divide is divmod).
  4. Counter (upper-right): counts accepted characters; converts the first negative status into the answer position; end-of-stream with zero status emits 0.

Micro-placement: the stack room was widened by one cell to shorten the pop loop from 18 to 16 ticks; to keep the 16×16 footprint the input room moved to the corner behind a 3-cell bent pipe. Official 26/26, average 184.1 public ticks.

Ours. The 22×22 line (377K, landed 1.286×) — we fought at footprint 22 vs 23 while they sat at 16 with a register-packed stack. The base-3-stack-in-a-register idea removes the storage pipes that forced our size.

reverse-a-list — 4.2× (20.0K vs 84.4K)

Problem. Read N values per round; emit them reversed.

Their committed prototype of note — the Y delay-line (catalog #37): one dispatcher man per round Y-splits per value; the east-born child parks the value in a private counted loop (8 ticks per remaining item, counted in BP), the west-born child returns in 6 ticks to split again; later values therefore finish waiting first, and the single output pipe receives the list already reversed — no per-value rooms, no collector network. Their prototype measured 130.0 public ticks against their then-best 171.6 but stood 22×69 (“too tall, needs folding”) — committed as a design with a note, alongside U-demux/token-handshake solutions in the trail (110K → 100K committed; a mid-trail note records official 47,059 and the policy “push only after reaching rank 1”; snapshot 20,026).

The convergence worth recording: our teammate gpt built the same Y-farm architecture during the contest and proved it correct, but its delays were SPATIAL (worker i waits by walking, Σ(D−2i) = W² cells) and it could not fit the winning box. Our own claude_42 note identified the repair — “counted loops instead of distance” — hours before the end. Unagi’s prototype is that exact repair, implemented: delay in TIME (backpack counter), not space.

Ours. 84.4K at snapshot via chatgpt_1’s landed 1.349×.

tcp — 1.7× (821K vs 1.36M) — our closest engineered gap

Problem. Reassemble a packet stream: emit values in sequence order, −1 for each packet outside the 16-wide window.

Their machine — 16×17. Future packets as (seq, val) pairs in a circulating FIFO; next-needed sequence in register B. The out-of-window test is branch-free: copy seq − next to the backpack and right-shift by 4 — any nonzero result means ≥16, emit −1 (catalog #28 + #9). Scan sentinel is 0 (never a valid sequence number). The capacity ledger is explicit and exact: 2 cells outbound + a 28-cell folded return + 1 integer held in the forwarder’s hand = 31 integers = 15 packets × 2 + sentinel — registers and pipe cells budgeted as one storage pool. The main room’s comparison/reinsertion column was moved left (11 → 10 columns) by resetting the backpack via the comparison’s own 0 result and re-saving through an edge b — same state machine, one column thinner. Verified with 128 random valid + 64 loss cases.

Ours. 1.36M — codex’s tcp line with the router optimization; our closest gap among seriously-engineered problems, and the clearest case of “same idea family, one protocol trick apart.”

memory — 1.6× (8.85M vs 14.0M)

Problem. A random-access memory service over pipe storage.

Their trail. Iterative packing with recorded numbers: wip 11.43M → 9.49M “packed to 22×22” → a 21×21 note; and a REJECTED experiment documented with its measurement — parallelizing the FIFO rotation with 2 workers (split n/2, (n−1)/2 so the second skips the loop when negative) targeting 4 ticks/item: no average gain, because consecutive-access-heavy cases regress. Recorded, then abandoned.

Ours. 14.0M, including the user’s own hand-edit (12 cells moved in the visual editor, −2.71% ticks) — the single event that seeded our whole “optimize rooms preserving function” manifest. This is the problem where our method (careful local moves under contracts) most resembled theirs; they simply ran more iterations of it.

sort-numbers — 2.5× (320K vs 802K)

Problem. Sort each round’s values ascending.

Their machine — 13×13 selection-sort ring. Each pass carries the remaining count as a header (catalog #10 — no sentinel value stolen from the domain); the candidate minimum rides in B; X sign-branches on the difference; non-candidates recirculate. Duplicates and the domain edges ±10000 need no special cases. The committed micro-step: bend the zero (equal) branch WEST into the same + that the positive path uses to restore x, then a dedicated adjacent s, rejoining the shared scan-count decrement — 4 ticks saved per equal comparison, footprint unchanged. Verified with a 200-round, 1,716-element multi-round session on both engines. Committed 377K; snapshot 320K.

Ours. 802K (1.012× late gain). Same algorithm family; theirs is one protocol generation tighter.

history-lesson — the problem we won (their 6,889 vs our 6,561)

Problem. Output a fixed 2,810-byte ASCII text (footprint-only scoring — ticks don’t matter).

Their machine — 83×82. The text compiled to 3,020 base-34 tokens (lowercase alphabet + 2 escapes + 5 frequent phrases); tokens packed 10–12 per signed 64-bit backtick literal; the GENERATOR validates every packed integer fits 64 bits read from either direction and lays 4 integers per two-row group with literal widths matched so no accidental vertical backtick pair can form — generation-time control of the column-pairing rule. A three-stage decoder (base-34 remainders → escape/phrase expansion → ASCII) sits beneath the data block; two decoder rooms were tucked beside an existing 2-cell pipe to shave the height 84 → 82.

Ours — 6,561, rank 21 vs their 28. Our history tokenizer line (phrase dictionary + packed literals, claude_35) found a slightly better encoding/geometry product. On the one problem scored purely on the axis our tooling was strongest at — literal packing under the backtick rules — we beat them.

triangle — parity (832 = 832)

The warm-up (triangular numbers). Both teams at the shared optimum 832; rank 59 vs 118 is submission-time tie-breaking. Unagi kept no solutions directory for it.

2. The convergences — same physics, different clocks

Reading their repo after building ours is an exercise in recognition. Independently, both teams derived:

  • The scoring calculus: judge by footprint² × ticks product; only a fully cleared perimeter band changes the footprint term (their optimizer roadmap; our claude_44).
  • The four ways a moved cell breaks: heading, nearest-pipe binding, timing/phase, literal pairing (their in-room rewrite semantics reference; our room_reflow/room_shrink checks).
  • Geometry as ABI: their matmul router verifies 40 nearest-pipe sites; our room_ports/binding_map machinery does the same check.
  • Roominess: their survey (snake 11.4% dense, gradebook 6.8%); our pathfinder room-0 measurement (2.26%).
  • Scored snapshots: their <score>.man convention and catalog #34; our subdb.
  • Best-only economics: their memorizer floor and free-submission play; our “submitting is free” doctrine.
  • The optimizer manifest: their optimize tool is nearly clause for clause the “randomized highly performing Rust room optimizer with path-aware transformations” our team dictated mid-contest — theirs was running by 07:43 UTC day 3; ours became room_ir/room_place/ room_compile after the end.
  • The Y delay-line: our gpt built the architecture, our claude_42 named the missing repair (counted delay, not spatial), their prototype implements exactly that repair.

The difference is never what was understood; it is when the machinery existed. They carried tools in; we derived tools live.

3. What they did that we genuinely did not

  1. Re-chose algorithms under process pressure. Six problems, ≥100× each — pathfinder (3 generations), matmul (2), subset-sum (enumeration → MITM), sudoku (bit-packing), plotter (measured cost model + planar re-plan), gradebook (roster ring). The scheduler’s rewrite mode institutionalizes this.
  2. Owned a compiler at contest time. Generators + routers + finalizers per problem; hand actors with room unit tests; chokudai’s netlist CAD flow with nearest-port contract checking.
  3. Bought percent-scale gains with idle CPU. The equivalence-gated annealer, auto-submitting, running unattended.
  4. Built knowledge for retrieval, not narrative. Catalog + textbook + postmortem template; an agent (or human) mid-problem can look up “how do I demux without tags” (#19) or “how do I control vertical literals” (#30).
  5. Isolated the API key in a service and versioned every submission with its event history.

4. What we did that held up

  • history-lesson: won outright (6,561 vs 6,889) — our tokenizer + literal-packing line was world-class on the footprint-only problem.
  • tcp, memory, sort within 1.6–2.5× — protocol-level engineering competitive with theirs.
  • Operational throughput at the end: 14 live improvements in the final hours through one judged submission relay, with ten of them being other agents’ finished work — the handoff topology worked.
  • Honest negative results discipline mirrored theirs: our verified no-gos (llm line-merge deadlock, gradebook shape search) parallel their recorded rejections (memory 2-worker rotation).
  • Post-contest convergence: our room compiler reached working form — rooms 4/5/3/1 of pathfinder compiled and verified in-machine — demonstrating the same architecture their tooling embodies.

5. Lessons folded forward (for 2027)

  1. Algorithm first, grid second — with a forcing function. Adopt the 50/50 improve/rewrite split and open every solution document with the computation graph and its paper tick count.
  2. Arrive with the factory. Extract this year’s judge/subdb/board scripts + the room compiler into a task-independent skeleton; rehearse once on a 2026 problem before the 2027 start.
  3. Conformance from hour one. Local engine + official engine behind one CLI over one shared case file; divergence catalog as a living document.
  4. Generators as the deliverable. Commit <score>.py next to every artifact; hand edits are for the frontier only, and get folded back into the generator the same day.
  5. Run the annealer as background income. Equivalence-gated modifiers over valid artifacts, auto-submitting; ~1–6% per problem is a rank fraction at the margin.
  6. Leaderboard-driven scheduling with expected-gain sampling (2 − points weighting) for grind-shaped work; keep our human-relay handoffs for design-shaped work.
  7. Knowledge as a catalog, one technique per entry with named examples — grow it during the contest, not after.
  8. Floors are free points: when a problem splits public/private cases, ship the honest memorizer while the real solution builds.

6. Verdict

Unagi’s edge decomposes cleanly: (a) six algorithm rewrites worth 100×–37,600× each — human insight, scheduled and forced by process; (b) a compiler stack that made every machine regenerable, every constraint (nearest-pipe, literal pairing, capacity) checked at generation time; (c) infrastructure and knowledge systems amortized across years. Our team matched them on physics understanding, on operational tempo, and on one problem outright; we lost where we let the first working algorithm stand. The room compiler we built after the contest — and their entire repository, which reads as its independently-evolved twin — both point at the same 2027 doctrine: generate, verify, anneal; and never stop asking whether the computation itself should change.
